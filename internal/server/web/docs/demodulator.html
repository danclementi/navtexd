1. Input and down-conversion (DDC)

What comes in: one of two kinds of signal.

  • Complex I/Q from an SDR (SDRconnect, SpyServer, HPSDR) or a stereo WAV. It carries a wide slice of spectrum, for example 250 kS/s around 500 kHz.
  • Real audio from a receiver's audio output, a mono WAV or a virtual audio cable. The receiver's LO sits a known distance below the NAVTEX frequency (input.device.lo_below_hz and the equivalent for the other sources), so the signal appears as an audio tone pair at about that offset.

What the DDC does: it turns either kind into a narrow, low-rate complex signal centred on the channel (channel.frequency_hz).

  1. Mix. Multiply by a complex oscillator so the target frequency (518 kHz, say) moves to 0 Hz. For real audio, first convert it to a complex (analytic) signal, then mix the offset away.
  2. Low-pass. A FIR filter removes everything outside about ±2 kHz. Without it, decimating would fold other stations and noise on top of the signal (aliasing).
  3. Decimate to about ddc.target_rate_hz = 4000 Hz, about 40 samples per 100-baud symbol.

Why: everything after this works on a tiny, cheap stream. The 250 kS/s input shrinks about 60-fold, and nothing outside ±2 kHz of the channel can disturb the later stages.

1.1 The DDC filter is a cascade

There is no single ±2 kHz filter. The decimation is a cascade of FIR stages, each a Kaiser design with 60 dB stop-band attenuation. Each stage passes up to 40% of its output rate and starts rejecting at 60%. The decimation factor is the integer nearest to input rate ÷ ddc.target_rate_hz whose prime factors are all 13 or smaller, so the rate after the DDC is not always exactly 4000 Hz.

2. Finding and tracking the carrier

After the DDC the signal is two tones 170 Hz apart. Their midpoint, the carrier, is not at 0 Hz: the transmitter's frequency error, the receiver's clock error and the tuning step all move it by some tens of Hz. If the demodulator assumed 0 Hz, the frequency measurements of step 3 would be biased towards one tone. Two mechanisms work on different time scales.

2.1 Initial search (comb search)

The receiver takes the last receiver.search_window_s (4 s) of signal and computes its spectrum. It slides a template with two peaks 170 Hz apart across ±receiver.search_range_hz (400 Hz) and picks the offset where the two peaks add up strongest. Only the pair spacing is used, so noise and other stations rarely fool it. A result is accepted only if the comb peak stands receiver.search_min_contrast (2.5) above the median (noise is about 1.2, signals 3 or more). The search is coarse: a few Hz of error is normal, and on some signals it sits about 20 Hz from where the tracker settles.

2.2 When the search runs

It does not run continuously.

  • No carrier known: every receiver.search_interval_s (1 s).
  • Carrier known (locked): only every receiver.search_verify_s (8 s), as a check. It retunes only if the new position is 25 Hz or more away, its score is at least 1.5 times that of the current position, and the contrast is accepted. The search itself always runs; the veto below only suppresses the retune that would follow it.
  • Veto: receiver.search_verify_veto_hz (30 Hz). If the tracker has reported healthy symbols within the last 6 s, the check may not retune. This fixed false retunes seen on good signals (0 = never veto).
  • Carrier forgotten: after receiver.lose_carrier_after_s (10 s) without timing lock, the search resumes every second.

2.3 Fine tracking, and what a cluster is

Each symbol the demodulator delivers is a single number: the average frequency over that 10 ms, in Hz relative to the assumed carrier (the soft value, step 4). A mark symbol ideally gives about +85 Hz and a space symbol about −85 Hz. Plot a few hundred of them on a number line and they pile up in two groups, one around +85 and one around −85. In noise each group spreads out, but the two stay distinct on a reasonably strong signal. These two groups are the clusters.

If the assumed carrier is wrong by +20 Hz, both clusters shift together: one sits near +65 and the other near −105. The midpoint between them is the carrier error. The tracker measures it like this:

  1. It takes the last receiver.track_window_symbols (300) locked soft values and splits them by sign.
  2. It takes the median of each group, the middle value when sorted. The median is used rather than the mean so that a few outliers, such as FM clicks (step 3), do not drag the estimate.
  3. The correction is half the sum of the two medians. In the example, (+65 + −105) / 2 = −20 Hz. It applies receiver.track_gain (0.7) of that, limited to receiver.track_max_step_hz (20 Hz) per update.

Safeguards: an update needs receiver.track_min_each_sign (60) symbols of each sign and receiver.track_min_symbols (200) in all; updates come every receiver.track_interval_symbols (100) new locked symbols; and the carrier never strays more than receiver.track_max_excursion_hz (30 Hz) from where the search placed it, because a tracker fed by noise can walk away until it sits on one tone. The tracker only learns from timing-locked symbols, so timing lock (step 4) comes first.

Health check. The same two medians give the "healthy" test used by the veto: both clusters at least 0.4 × 85 Hz (about 34 Hz) from zero, meaning the tones are clearly separated, and the midpoint within receiver.search_verify_veto_hz of the current carrier.

Where it breaks down. In noise alone there are no real clusters: the values scatter around zero and the medians mean nothing. A long run of one tone (idle or phasing signal) fills one group much more than the other; the medians are still computed correctly, but the estimate rests on the smaller group and can be less accurate.

A carrier estimate wrong by 20 Hz costs some sensitivity, since the tones then sit off-centre in the narrow filter, but does not stop decoding. A large error does, which is what the 8 s check exists to fix.

3. Narrow filter and frequency discriminator

The demodulator does not run a mark filter and a space filter and compare their energies. It measures the instantaneous frequency of the signal directly: an FM discriminator.

  1. Move the carrier to 0 Hz. The 4 kS/s complex signal is multiplied by a rotating phasor at the current carrier estimate. Mark and space now sit at about ±85 Hz, half of receiver.demod.shift_hz (170).
  2. Narrow low-pass filter, cutoff receiver.demod.channel_cutoff_hz (170 Hz). It keeps both tones and removes noise, neighbouring stations and DDC leftovers beyond about 220 Hz. It matters more than it sounds, because the next stage is very sensitive to noise: this filter sets how much noise gets through.
  3. Discriminator. Multiply each sample by the complex conjugate of the previous sample. The angle of the product is how far the phase advanced in one sample; multiplied by fs/2π, it is the instantaneous frequency in Hz. A clean mark gives about +85 Hz, a clean space about −85 Hz (which is which is the polarity, decoder.polarity). In noise the value scatters around ±85 Hz with occasional large spikes (FM "clicks"), so it is clipped to ±1.5 times half the shift (±127 Hz) to keep a click from dominating.

The same filter taps also run on two extra copies of the signal, shifted to ±receiver.demod.presence_offset_hz (350 Hz) from the carrier. They are not decoded; their power is compared with the carrier band in step 4 to decide whether a signal is present at all.

What comes out: a frequency estimate for every sample, 4000 per second. A symbol lasts 10 ms, about 40 samples, so each symbol is represented by about 40 noisy values.

3.1 The filter itself

A linear-phase FIR designed by the window method: a sinc low-pass with a Kaiser window, normalised to unity gain at DC. It follows scipy.signal.firwin(n, cutoff, window=("kaiser", beta)) and the reference narrow_filter of the navtex-rs/bgm60 code. It is not a Remez design. The taps are not tuned by hand; only the cutoff is an option.

  • The cutoff is the −6 dB point, as with any window-method sinc. At 170 Hz the ±85 Hz tones are almost untouched.
  • The design asks for a 100 Hz transition width and 60 dB stop-band attenuation, both fixed in the code. These give Kaiser β = 0.1102 (60 − 8.7) ≈ 5.65 and a length N = ⌈(A − 7.95) / (2.285 · Δω)⌉ + 1, rounded up to an odd number, with Δω = 2π · 100 Hz / fs.
  • At 4000 Hz this is 147 taps (about 37 ms), with a whole-sample group delay of (N−1)/2. At 3968 Hz it is essentially the same.
  • The length depends on the sample rate, the transition width and the stop-band attenuation, not on the cutoff. Because the transition width and attenuation are fixed, changing receiver.demod.channel_cutoff_hz only moves where the transition band sits; it does not change the filter's sharpness or its time response. A wider transition (150 to 200 Hz) would need roughly 75 to 100 taps and ring less; a narrower one the opposite.

3.2 Filter bandwidth

A cutoff of 170 Hz was found to be a good compromise on test data. Much narrower filters smear the symbols into each other and weaken the timing measurement of step 4; wider ones let more noise through.

4. Presence, timing recovery and integrate-and-dump

Step 3 produced a frequency estimate for every sample. Now the demodulator decides whether a signal is there, where each 10 ms symbol starts and ends, and what single value represents each symbol. It works in blocks of receiver.demod.timing_block_symbols (100 symbols, 1 s).

4.1 Presence check

For each block it compares the power in the carrier band with the average power in the two comparison bands at ±receiver.demod.presence_offset_hz (350 Hz). If the carrier band exceeds them by at least receiver.demod.presence_db (1.5 dB), a signal is present. This stops the timing recovery from locking onto pure noise, which also has some baud-rate content by chance.

4.2 Timing measurement

The transmitter's symbol clock and ours are unrelated, so the symbol boundaries must be measured.

  • For each sample it forms a weight that is large when the instantaneous frequency is near one of the two tones and dips towards zero when the frequency passes near the centre during a mark/space transition (signal amplitude times the square of the frequency normalised to half the shift).
  • Over a block that weight forms a ripple at the symbol rate, with dips at symbol edges where the tone changes. The demodulator correlates it with a 100 Hz rotating phasor. The phase of the result is where in the 40-sample symbol the edges fall: the timing offset.
  • The magnitude of the result divided by the total weight is the timing confidence: how strongly the baud-rate ripple stands out. A block is good if the confidence is at least receiver.demod.timing_confidence (0.015) and the presence check passed.
  • The measure only sees symbol edges where the tone changes. A signal with few transitions, or a filter so narrow that it smears them, gives low confidence.

4.3 Lock and tracking

  • The first good block locks the timing at its measured offset.
  • Each later good block corrects it with a first-order loop: new offset = old + receiver.demod.timing_gain (0.4) × error. This follows the small clock difference between transmitter and receiver.
  • After receiver.demod.timing_lost_blocks (3) consecutive bad blocks, timing lock is dropped.
  • While unlocked, symbols are output as zero with "not locked", and the carrier tracker of step 2 ignores them.

4.4 Integrate-and-dump

With the timing known, each symbol window is exactly one symbol long, centred on the measured position. The demodulator adds up all the discriminator products (the complex numbers, before taking the angle) inside the window and takes the angle of the sum. That angle, converted to Hz, is the symbol's soft value, clipped to ±1.5 times half the shift.

  • Adding the complex products before taking the angle weights strong samples more than weak ones, so it is more robust against a single noisy sample than averaging the frequencies.
  • Window edges fall between samples, so partial samples are weighted. This also handles the 250 kS/s case where the rate after the DDC is 3968 Hz, so a symbol is 39.68 samples.
  • Symbols are released only after the whole block has been analysed and the samples of the window have arrived, so the output lags the input by about 1 to 2 s.

What comes out: one soft value per symbol, in Hz, with a "locked" flag. Positive means the higher tone; zero means no lock.

5. Bits, characters and the repeat (SITOR-B / CCIR 476)

The input is a stream of soft values, one per 10 ms, in Hz (0 when unlocked). The output is characters.

5.1 From soft value to bit

The sign of the soft value is the bit: the higher tone is mark, which is 1. Which tone is mark depends on whether the receiver is in USB or LSB, so decoder.polarity (auto) tries both readings in parallel. Each soft value is also normalised by half of decoder.shift_hz and clipped to ±1.5, which keeps the confidence information for the combining step.

5.2 The code

Every character is 7 bits and exactly four of them are 1: 35 valid codewords out of 128 patterns. A word with any other number of 1s is certainly damaged. Random noise produces a valid word only 27% of the time (35/128), so a run of valid words is strong evidence of real data.

5.3 Finding the word boundaries (framing), decoder.framing = hard

The bit stream has no markers, so there are seven possible alignments.

  • For every alignment and both polarities the decoder counts consecutive valid words. Ten in a row (decoder.lock_words) gives a lock; by chance that is very unlikely (0.27 to the power 10).
  • The lock ends after decoder.unlock_bad_words (8) invalid words in a row.
  • After a fade it re-locks on only decoder.relock_words (4) valid words, but only at the same alignment and polarity and only within decoder.relock_window_s (120 s). The bit clock runs on through a fade, so the old alignment is still right.

5.4 The repeat (time diversity)

Each character is sent twice. The stream interleaves new characters (the DX copy) and repeats of earlier ones (the RX copy). The repeat comes five character-times later, 350 ms, so a noise burst would have to be longer than that to damage both copies. The receiver must also find out which slots carry the first copy and which the repeat. It compares word pairs and waits until decoder.parity_votes (10) pairs agree, with decoder.parity_factor (3) times more votes than the other parity. After a re-lock at the same alignment within decoder.keep_parity_symbols (3000 symbols) it reuses the earlier answer.

5.5 Combining the two copies

The decoder adds the soft values of the two copies bit by bit and picks the valid codeword that fits best (maximum likelihood over the 35 codewords). A bit that was weak in one copy is decided by the other, instead of discarding either copy. Special cases:

  • Both copies valid but different: two separate signals, not a repeat. This is what the phasing signal looks like. The first copy is kept.
  • Neither copy valid: the character is unreadable. decoder.unreadable decides what is printed: ~ (as YaND does), *, or the nearest valid character.

5.6 Code to text

The code has letters and figures shifts, and the decoder tracks the current shift. decoder.unshift_on_space (off) makes a space return to letters, which helps when a shift character was missed. The bit order within the 7 bits is decoder.bit_order; in auto mode it shows LSB first until it finds ZCZC, which only decodes correctly in the right order, and then commits.

5.7 Soft framing, decoder.framing = soft

Soft framing replaces the framing step. It does not wait for ten clean words. For all alignments it continuously scores how well the soft values 35 bits apart (the repeat) match each other, and locks when the evidence accumulated over the recent window reaches a statistical threshold. That is why it works in more noise, follows bit slips and station changes, and locks later. It also shows ! where the two copies each looked valid but different. It takes the bit order as LSB, ignores decoder.unshift_on_space, and does not use decoder.lock_words, decoder.unlock_bad_words, decoder.parity_votes, decoder.parity_factor, decoder.keep_parity_symbols or decoder.relock_words. Changing the framing restarts the channel.

What comes out: a plain text stream, with ~ where nothing could be recovered.

6. From text to messages

Up to now there is only a character stream. The last stage cuts it into NAVTEX messages, drops junk and repairs words.

6.1 Message structure

  • ZCZC starts a message.
  • The identification group follows: B1 the transmitting station letter, B2 the subject (navigational warning, weather forecast, and so on), B3B4 a serial number 01 to 99 (00 = urgent).
  • Then the text, and NNNN ends it. The station sends BRK before a new ZCZC, so BRK is stripped from an interrupted message.

6.2 Segmentation (segment.*)

The segmenter turns the stream into records of three kinds: a message (ZCZC to NNNN), an incomplete message (cut short by the next ZCZC, by silence or by the end of the stream; output.incomplete_messages, on, decides whether these are reported) and a fragment (text outside any message, mostly noise on a weak channel).

  • Damaged markers: a ZCZC with up to two unreadable characters, or one missing ("ZCZ~", "CZC"), still starts a message, provided an identification group (two letters, two figures) follows. An NNNN with one unreadable character ("NN~N") also counts, but only inside a message, where it is far more likely than anything else.
  • Silence: a record with no new character for segment.idle_close_s (60 s) is closed.
  • Long lines: a line break is forced after segment.max_line_chars (200) characters without one. decoder.break_before_zczc and decoder.break_after_brk insert breaks around ZCZC and BRK so the layout looks like YaND's.
  • Each line keeps the time it was sent and the signal quality at that moment.

6.3 Dropping noise fragments

A fragment is kept only if it passes these tests. Complete messages are never dropped by them.

  • At least segment.min_fragment_chars (16) visible characters.
  • Optionally (segment.min_fragment_words, 0 = off): at least that many real words of segment.min_fragment_word_letters (4) letters or more. segment.fragment_words_vocabulary (auto) decides whether "real" means found in the maritime vocabulary (on at 518 kHz; off at 490 and 424 kHz, where the vocabulary does not fit the language) or merely a run of letters. The vocabulary holds words of 5 letters or more, so with it on, shorter words do not count. segment.fragment_words_after_autocorrect (on) counts words after the repair below.

6.4 Autocorrect (autocorrect.*)

A word containing unreadable characters ("WARN~NG") is looked up in the maritime vocabulary. It is a candidate only if it has autocorrect.min_word_letters (5) to autocorrect.max_word_letters (16) letters and at most autocorrect.max_unreadable_percent (50%) of its characters are unreadable. If one vocabulary word fits, it is used. If up to autocorrect.max_candidates (4) fit, the output shows them as [A|B|C]. If more fit, the word is left alone. The raw text is never changed: the corrected text is stored beside it. autocorrect.extra_words_file (vocabulary.txt) adds your own words and is re-read when it changes. The whole stage can be switched off with autocorrect.enabled.

6.5 Output

output.format selects time-stamped text, parsed messages or one JSON object per message.

Summary and known weak spots

The whole chain: audio or I/Q → DDC to about 4 kS/s → carrier search and tracking → narrow filter and FM discriminator → presence and timing, integrate-and-dump into soft symbols → bits, framing, the repeat combined → characters → ZCZC…NNNN messages, cleaned up and repaired.

Places where the clips and the sweep showed real weaknesses:

  • Timing lock on balanced signals (step 4): the timing metric is weak on centred, balanced signals and gets worse with a narrower channel filter. The default receiver.demod.timing_confidence was lowered to 0.015 because of this.
  • Carrier search bias (step 2): the comb search tends to give about −3.5 Hz while the tracker settles at +15 to +20 Hz on the same signal. This is why the retune check can be vetoed by the tracker.
  • Soft lock in noise (step 5): the soft framing can emit random valid-looking characters in noise. Weak-character gating has been left out on purpose, to keep the logic close to the original.
